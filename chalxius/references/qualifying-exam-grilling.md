# Qualifying Exam Grilling

Use this mode to turn syllabi and past papers into an adaptive preparation
program. Optimize for independent, timed proof production rather than familiarity.

## Contents

- Intake and assessment integrity
- Baseline and training modes
- Evidence scoring and spaced regrilling
- Eight-week schedule
- Durable progress ledger

## Intake one fact at a time

Resolve these items in order, first from artifacts and safe defaults:

1. Confirm how many subjects are required and which subject, if any, is fixed by
   the advisor or program.
2. Confirm the candidate pool and then the exact subjects being taken.
3. Obtain the exam date; if unknown, use the stated remaining time provisionally.
4. Obtain realistic study hours per day and days per week.
5. Identify which past papers and solutions the user has already seen.
6. Ask about recent coursework, strongest area, and most feared area only when the
   answer changes the first diagnostic.

Ask only about the next unanswered item whose answer would materially change the
diagnostic or schedule. Do not turn intake into a form, and do not ask about
reversible administrative choices.

If the user reports two months, start with an eight-week provisional horizon and
recalculate when an exact date is available.

If a personal exam profile contains a user-confirmed current rule, trust that
rule over older public pages. Do not ask the user to reconfirm it unless they
report that the rule may have changed.

## Protect assessment integrity

- Inventory the source directory before training. Use the newest syllabus as the
  scope authority and past papers as evidence of style, not as the whole syllabus.
- Reserve at least two unseen, recent subject papers or equivalent problem sets
  for timed mocks. Record the reserve before revealing their questions.
- Keep answer keys and worked solutions sealed until the user has attempted the
  problem and exhausted the agreed hint level.
- Preserve the original wording and cite year, subject, question, and PDF page.
  Visually verify formulas when text extraction is uncertain.
- Do not overfit repeated recent questions. Allocate roughly 50% of training to
  high-frequency patterns, 30% to full syllabus coverage, and 20% to unfamiliar
  transfer problems; adapt these ratios to diagnostic evidence.

## Establish the baseline

Run one closed-book diagnostic problem per confirmed subject, one problem at a
time. Prefer representative recent problems that are not in the reserved set.
Measure:

- theorem and definition recall,
- choice of strategy,
- proof completeness,
- algebraic or computational accuracy,
- clarity under time pressure,
- number and depth of hints.

Do not infer mastery from self-report. Use produced work as evidence.

## Use four training modes

1. **Diagnostic**: sample dependencies and locate the earliest broken prerequisite.
2. **Drill**: question one definition, proof step, computation, or transfer at a
   time; use the normal staged hint ladder.
3. **Timed mock**: with explicit consent, give an intact 50-point subject section
   or agreed subset. Give no hints until submission; an accepted hint converts the
   item from mock evidence into practice.
4. **Repair and regrill**: classify the error, teach only the missing dependency,
   then require a blank-page reconstruction and a spaced variant.

## Close every item with a dependency-graph repair

Use this mandatory closure after each completed diagnostic, drill, mock item, or
abandoned proof branch:

`attempt -> node-level diagnosis -> targeted supplement -> dependency-graph update
-> blank-page reconstruction -> spaced transfer variant`

- Decompose the item into atomic knowledge nodes rather than assigning only one
  whole-problem score. Typical nodes are definitions, object constructions,
  theorem hypotheses, theorem conclusions, proof mechanisms, strategy triggers,
  computations, examples, counterexamples, and misconception repairs.
- Give every node explicit prerequisite edges. Multiple prerequisites are allowed;
  distinguish "must know first" from a merely related topic.
- For each affected node record: current mastery level 0–5, direct evidence,
  hint depth, dominant error tag, last-tested date, and next-due date. Raise
  mastery only from user production. Explanations supplied by the examiner create
  a taught-but-untested state, not independent evidence.
- The supplement should repair the earliest broken dependency, state the exact
  conditions of any invoked theorem, and expose the connection that the user
  failed to trigger. Add one discriminating example, counterexample, or transfer
  cue when useful. Do not replace the drill with a survey lecture.
- When an existing fact graph is available, read its static artifacts and store
  attempts, misconceptions, teaching nodes, examples, and review plans in the
  separate learning overlay. Maintain prerequisite edges and mastery metadata.
  Only source facts already admitted by the external graph may be treated as
  proof premises; a learner node is never called verified. Do not invoke
  a Chalxius research work unit or multi-agent proof work from the drill.
- End the closure with exactly one reconstruction or transfer question. Do not
  advance to a new branch until the user can at least restate the repaired link,
  unless they explicitly choose to defer it.

## Score evidence consistently

Assign each topic an evidence level:

- **0 Unseen**: no attempt.
- **1 Recognized**: identifies the topic but cannot start.
- **2 Scaffolded**: solves only with a proof skeleton or multiple hints.
- **3 Prompted**: solves with one small hint or a material correction.
- **4 Independent**: correct, complete untimed solution without hints.
- **5 Exam-ready**: correct, complete timed solution plus a nearby transfer
  problem on a later day.

Also tag the dominant error: definition, theorem recall, strategy, logical gap,
calculation, scope, time management, or exposition. Never average away a critical
prerequisite gap.

## Schedule regrilling

- Revisit levels 0–2 or misconceptions after about 1, 3, 7, and 14 days.
- Revisit level 3 after about 3, 7, and 14 days.
- Revisit level 4 after about 7, 14, and 28 days with a variant.
- Keep level 5 warm through mixed timed sets.

Prioritize items that are simultaneously weak, central to many proofs, and common
in the source papers. Adjust intervals when evidence shows faster forgetting.

## Use the eight-week default

- **Week 1**: intake, source inventory, reserve selection, brief candidate
  screening when selection is unfinished, and a baseline for the required
  subjects.
- **Weeks 2–3**: repair definitions and central theorem chains; combine oral
  reconstruction with short written proofs.
- **Weeks 4–5**: interleave authentic past-paper patterns with unseen variants;
  introduce timed half-sections.
- **Week 6**: run full 50-point subject sections and repair presentation and time
  allocation.
- **Week 7**: use the first reserved mock, then target only errors supported by
  its evidence.
- **Week 8**: use the final reserved mock early, regrill high-risk dependencies,
  rehearse theorem maps, and taper rather than cramming new peripheral material.

Compress phases proportionally when less time remains. Do not skip the baseline,
the reserved mock, or post-mock repair.

## Maintain a durable ledger

Ask once whether the user wants persistent tracking and where it should live.
Otherwise keep a compact in-thread ledger. Record the problem attempt as:

`date | source/problem | affected node ids | time | hints | problem evidence |
error tag | next due`

Keep node-level mastery, prerequisite edges, evidence, and due dates in the
knowledge-dependency graph rather than squeezing them into the attempt row.

At each checkpoint, show the weakest dependency, the strongest timed evidence,
overdue reviews, and one next question. Declare readiness only after repeated,
spaced, timed evidence across the confirmed syllabus.
